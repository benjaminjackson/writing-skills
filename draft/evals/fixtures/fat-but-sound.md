It is important to note that we stopped estimating work in story points last year, and I want to explain the reasoning behind that particular decision.

The fundamental issue with points is that they are, in essence, a prediction dressed up as a measurement. When a team says a piece of work is five points, what they are really and truly saying is that it feels somewhat bigger than a three and rather smaller than an eight. That is a genuinely useful signal for a conversation. The problem arises when that signal gets aggregated into a velocity number, at which point it starts to look like data.

Once it looks like data, it gets used like data. In our case, this basically meant that velocity showed up in a quarterly review deck, and from that point forward it was no longer possible to have an honest conversation about whether a piece of work was actually a five, because the number had consequences attached to it.

What we do now is quite simple. We ask one question, which is whether the work fits in a week. If the answer is no, we split it until the answer is yes. This is not more accurate than points. It is, however, considerably harder to game, and it fails loudly rather than quietly when we get it wrong.

At the end of the day, the thing we lost was a chart. The thing we gained was engineers who will tell you when something is bigger than it looks.
