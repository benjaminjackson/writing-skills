# Why we moved code review to the morning

## The waiting problem

Last spring our team of nine engineers measured where a change spent its time between the first commit and the merge. We expected the build to be the slow part, since a full run of the test suite takes about twenty minutes. It is worth pointing out that most of the time a change spent waiting was spent in review, not in CI. A typical pull request sat for a day and a half before anyone opened it, and by the time the comments arrived the author had usually moved on to other work. Switching back to an old branch cost each of us an hour of rereading our own code, and a second round of comments often meant a second day of waiting. Over a month, the waiting added up to more time than the building, the testing and the deploying put together.

Nobody on the team was careless about reviews. The trouble was timing. Review requests arrived all day, and each one landed in the middle of somebody's own work. A reviewer deep in a refactor would see the notification, decide to finish the function first, and then forget the request until the next morning. Small changes waited as long as large ones, because the wait had nothing to do with the size of the change and everything to do with when it arrived. We tried a rotating reviewer of the day, which helped for a week and then faded, since the person on duty still had their own tickets due. We tried a bot that posted a reminder after four hours, and people learned to ignore the bot. Each fix treated the reviewer as the problem, when the real problem was that a review had no set place in anyone's day. A reviewer who wanted to help still had to choose, every time, between their own deadline and somebody else's, and their own deadline usually won. The author, meanwhile, could see the pull request sitting there with no comments and no way to know whether anyone had even looked at it. Some authors pinged a reviewer directly, which worked once or twice and then made people feel hounded.

## The morning block

In June we tried a fixed block instead. From nine to ten each morning, nobody writes code. The first hour of the day belongs to the queue of open pull requests, oldest first, and anyone who empties the queue early can go back to their own work. We picked the morning because the queue is at its longest then, after a full day of requests has piled up, and because most of us arrive with no half-finished thought to protect. Authors learned to open their pull requests before they left in the evening, so the change would sit at the front of the next morning's queue. The block also gave reviewers permission to ignore review notifications for the rest of the day, which most of them had wanted for a long time. For the first two weeks we kept a shared document where people wrote down what went wrong with the block, and most entries were small: a reviewer who overslept, a queue that ran past ten, a pull request too large to read in an hour. We answered the last one by asking authors to split any change that would take a reviewer more than twenty minutes to read.

The rule needed two adjustments in its first month. Urgent fixes for production could not wait until the next morning, so we kept a separate label for them that anyone may review at any hour. And the block had to cover any and all review requests, including the small ones, or people would quietly route their small changes around it by asking a friend for a quick approval in chat. Once every request went through the same queue, the queue became something the team could see and talk about at standup. In the retro, Odile said it best: "I review better before I have written any code of my own." Several people nodded, and the block stayed.

## What changed

Review waits got much shorter after the change. We did not set out to measure the change carefully, and we did not keep the spreadsheet from the spring, but nobody on the team doubts the direction. A pull request opened in the afternoon is now usually reviewed by the end of the next morning's block, and the author gets the comments while the change is still fresh. Second rounds got faster too, because the author can answer the comments the same day and the reviewer sees the revision in the next morning's block. The pull requests that sat untouched for days, which used to be a weekly complaint at standup, have almost disappeared. Authors also stopped splitting their attention across three open branches, since they could count on hearing back within a day. Fewer branches open at once meant fewer merge conflicts, and the conflicts that did happen were smaller, because no branch lived long enough to drift far from the main line. The release manager noticed the change before anyone told her, because the list of pull requests she had to chase at the end of each sprint got short enough to read at a glance.

The reviews themselves changed as well. A reviewer with an hour set aside reads the whole diff instead of skimming it between tasks, and the comments got longer and more useful. Newer engineers said they learned more from one careful review than from three hurried ones. Reviewers also started to notice patterns across pull requests, such as two people solving the same problem in different ways, because they read several changes back to back. Some of those patterns turned into shared helpers, and a few turned into short design discussions that would never have happened in a comment thread. Reviewers also got better at saying when a change was fine as it stood. Under the old habit, a reviewer who opened a pull request late in the day felt they owed the author at least one comment, and small requests for renaming piled up on changes that were already good. With an hour set aside and a queue to finish, reviewers approved clean changes quickly and saved their comments for the ones that needed them.

## What it cost

The block was not free. An hour a day is an eighth of every engineer's working time, and a few people resented giving their sharpest hour to other people's code. The morning block also moved the standup to ten, and it left the afternoon with no review slot. This made the late-day backlog worse. A change finished at two in the afternoon now waits until the next morning, where before it might have been reviewed by four. People who liked to finish a change and see it merged on the same day lost the same-day merge, and they said so.

We have kept the block for five months, and nobody has asked to drop it. The afternoon backlog is the cost we chose to accept, because a day of waiting at a known time is easier to plan around than a day and a half of waiting at an unknown one. If the team grows past twelve, we expect to split the queue by area of the code, so that one hour still covers it. Until then, the hour stays where it is, and the first thing anyone new learns on their first morning is to open the queue before they open their editor. Two new hires joined in the autumn, and both said the block was the easiest part of the team's routine to pick up.
