# Search Plan

## Where the search actually stands

You've had four real conversations in six weeks, and three of them came from people you already knew. That isn't luck and it isn't a small sample. It's the shape of almost every search at your level, and it's worth naming now because it decides where the next eight weeks go.

The fourth conversation, the one that came through the recruiter, is the one that's gone nowhere. You met a screener, then a second screener, and the role description moved twice between the two calls. I'd stop spending time there. Recruiter processes at this level are a lottery you can't influence, and you've now bought a ticket and watched the draw.

Your list has fourteen organizations on it. Ten have someone in your network within one hop. Four don't, and those four are the ones you keep opening the tab for, because they're the most interesting and the least reachable. That gap is the thing this plan has to solve, and everything below is about closing it.

Two of the fourteen have already been through a hiring cycle for the role you want and closed it in the last five months. I'd take them off. That leaves twelve live, ten of them reachable, and a list of twelve you can actually work is worth more than a list of fourteen you can admire.

## Why warm introductions do the work

A warm introduction is worth more than a strong application, and the reason is narrower than people usually give: it changes who reads you first.

An application enters a process, and a process starts with a screener whose job is to make a pile smaller. Very little of what you've done in the last eleven years survives that, because the reduction runs on titles and years and keywords, and your record doesn't sit in any of those boxes cleanly. The screener isn't making a mistake. They're doing the job they were given, which is to cut.

When someone inside the organization forwards your name, you skip the pile entirely. You show up as a person a colleague vouched for, and the first question in the room becomes "what's she like to work with" instead of "does this match the spec." Those are different questions and they get different answers.

The purpose of a professional network is to create durable channels of mutual value over an extended period. It is important to understand that such relationships are established through consistent engagement rather than through transactional outreach at the point of need. Organizations increasingly recognize that referred candidates demonstrate higher retention and stronger performance outcomes, and this recognition has meaningfully shifted hiring behavior across most sectors over the past decade.

It is therefore essential to approach each introduction with clarity regarding the value proposition being offered. A well-constructed request enables the intermediary to advocate effectively on the candidate's behalf, and reduces the burden placed upon them. Care should be taken to ensure that the recipient understands both the specific opportunity under discussion and the rationale for the connection being proposed.

The practical version of that is short. Write the introduction email yourself, in the third person, so the person forwarding it can paste it and add one line. Two paragraphs, no attachments. Say what you're looking for in one sentence and why this organization specifically in the next.

The other half is who you ask. People ask their strongest contacts first, which is backwards. Your strongest contacts have already offered, and they're already thinking about you. The value sits one ring out, with people who know you well enough to vouch and aren't close enough to have thought of you unprompted.

So a warm introduction beats an application, because of who reads your name first and what they ask about you.

## What happens next

The next eight weeks split into two halves that run at the same time, not one after the other.

The first half is the ten reachable organizations. One introduction request per week, in the order of how much you want them, starting third rather than first. The first two requests are where you find out that your one-sentence pitch is too long, and you'd rather learn that on your third choice than your first.

The second half is the four you can't reach. Reaching them isn't an introduction problem, it's a visibility problem, and the fix is slower. Two of them have a conference in the spring where a panel would put you in the room. One has a former colleague of yours on the board, two hops out, which is worth one careful email. The fourth I'd let go for now.

Track it in one place, and keep it boring. Organization, who you asked, when, what happened. Six weeks in you'll want to know which of your contacts actually forwards things, and you won't remember.

The thing to watch for is the month-three dip. Around week ten most searches go quiet at once, because the introductions you made in week two have all resolved and the ones from week eight haven't landed yet. It isn't a signal. It's arithmetic, and the only wrong response is to start applying cold to make the quiet stop.

Send me the twelve-organization list with the one-hop contacts named, and I'll tell you which three I'd start with.
